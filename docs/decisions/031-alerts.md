# 031. An alert of its own, set on spots

Status: accepted on 2026-10-11, and being built. Built: the alert and the settings in the API, the rules in `packages/conditions/src/alerts/`, the worker's look, which writes "found", "moved" and "gone", its lighter pass every quarter of an hour, which writes "it is on" and the evening before, and the web app's screens: the alerts of a spot, listed in the spot's panel and each said in one sentence, and what was told, the alerts and the settings under the bell. Since [decision 040](040-phone-navigation.md) the settings are a part of the account's panel, and the bell keeps what was told and the alerts. The mail is built too: a delivery beside each notification of an account that said yes, sent by the lighter pass after the account's quiet hours and written as it leaves, within the limits, with the link and the headers that stop it in one press. A spot takes several alerts since 2026-10-12. The worker sends push since that day too: a delivery beside each notification of an account that has a browser subscribed, sent to each of its browsers by the rules a mail leaves by, with what it tells kept apart from what mail told. The web app's part of push is built too: the instance's keys, the API that keeps an account's devices, the switch "on this device" with the list of devices, and the service worker. The app for phones has its tab of the alerts, the alerts of a place in its sheet and the editor since 2026-10-20 ([decision 069](069-an-app-for-phones.md)). It replaces [decision 008](008-alerts.md), whose rules Téo had not confirmed. He answered six questions that day, and what follows is built from his answers. Where a rule is not his but follows from one, the text says "chosen here": those are his to overturn.

## Context

Decision 008 kept an alert inside the spot: the spot's criteria, and a switch. Téo tried a screen that set both at once and asked for two things instead (decision 006, "A spot without its alert"): a spot is a place, and its alert is another object. He calls the alert the most important part of the product.

The first version also had faults that no rule of it named. It announced a window in the middle of the night. It counted days in UTC. It kept one window a day. It sent nothing: an alert was a line in a panel that had to be opened. And it could not say that a window had begun.

## What Téo said

- **An alert for a spot, or several.** On 2026-10-11 he chose one alert for a spot. The next day, with the screens in hand, he asked for several, each with its name and its conditions: one for small clean days, say, and one for big ones. Spots that lie side by side and work in about the same conditions may share one. An alert has a name.
- **When it speaks.** At three moments, each of which can be switched off: when a window takes shape and holds on two forecasts in a row; the evening before, in one message for all the spots of the next day; when an announced window goes or moves by more than two hours.
- **How far ahead** is a setting of the account, not of the alert.
- **Availability** is a setting of the account too: the alert stays quiet when its owner cannot go.
- **Strict conditions for now.** Later a window may come with a likelihood, "80% that it matches".
- **By mail**, with web push made ready.
- **"It is on"**: the alert says so when its window has begun, to remind its owner that it is live.
- **A setting for everything.**

## Decision

### The alert

- An alert belongs to one account, and only that account reads it. It has a name, the conditions that make its spots work, and a switch. The conditions are the criteria of decision 006: swell height, period and direction, wind speed and direction, and the tide, which is asked by its cycle and its coefficient since 2026-10-19 ("The tide"). A part may take other values ("Or").
- An alert is set on one spot or on several, all of its account, and a spot may be in several alerts. Since [decision 064](064-alerts-on-surf-breaks.md) it may be set on surf breaks of the catalogue too, which count among its twelve places. The conditions are checked at each of an alert's spots, with that spot's own forecast and tide: spots that share an alert share what is asked of the sea, not the sea. Two alerts on one spot are looked at, told and sent apart: each keeps what it saw and what it told there, and hours that both find are told by both, each under its name.
- An alert keeps the shortest window worth telling, two hours unless its owner says otherwise. Chosen here, and kept on the alert: a spot that works an hour around low tide is a matter of the spot, not of the person.
- An alert whose last spot is deleted, or taken out of it, is deleted with it.
- An account holds at most 100 alerts, and an alert at most 12 spots. Chosen here.
- A spot's panel lists its alerts, each with its name, what it asks and its switch, and opens one to change it. An alert is written in a sheet of its own, over whatever is open, and not in the panel, which Téo asked for on 2026-10-16: its name, the sentence of its conditions, what they give over the days ahead, and its button to save, which stays at the bottom of the sheet and closes it. A saved alert is turned off, copied and deleted there too. A second alert of a spot is named after the spot and its rank, and any alert can be started from a copy of another: one for small clean days is a few figures away from one for big ones. Chosen here.
- What is written of an alert and not saved is not lost without a word. Opening another alert, starting a copy, closing the sheet or the panel, choosing something else on the map, following a link, the browser's way back, signing out and leaving the page all ask first: one question at a time, in a dialog over whatever is open, and the alert that is open stays as it is when it is asked for again. What was sent is not saved until its save has answered, and a copy is something to lose before it is touched. What is written stays guarded until it is saved or its editor is gone: an answer to leave does not take the guard away, so a leaving that fails changes nothing. The browser's way back is undone at once and done again when the answer is to leave. Renaming or moving the spot keeps its panel as it was. Deleting the spot asks for itself, and nothing more. A part that is changed again while its save is on its way is kept as it was last written, and one that is put back to what the alert says follows the alert again.
- An alert asks at least one thing of the sea: with no condition every hour would be a window. A range with neither a least nor a most asks nothing, and is not kept.
- An alert starts over when its conditions change, when it is switched off, and at a spot that is moved: what it saw of the sea and what it told there are forgotten, and nothing is called off. The sea is another under other conditions, and at another place. Chosen here.
- `spot.criteria` and `spot.alerts_enabled` are no longer read. Each spot whose criteria were not empty gets an alert of its name, with those criteria and the spot's switch. A range with neither end is left out on the way, and a spot with no criteria gets no alert, whatever its switch said. The two columns stay one release more, for the version that still reads them while this one is deployed (decision 009), and the release after drops them. It first gives an alert to each spot that has criteria and no alert, which the version before may have made meanwhile. A change of criteria made through the version before while this one is deployed, to a spot that has its alert already, is not carried over: no screen ever set a spot's criteria. `v1` has not been released (decision 003), so the spot's answer loses the two fields, and `GET /v1/spots/{id}/conditions` becomes `POST /v1/alerts/preview`, which tries conditions at spots before they are saved.

One set for all the alerts of an account, each with a default so that an account that sets nothing is told sensibly.

- A setting changes what is told from then on. It calls nothing off and tells nothing again, and a change of settings is never told as a change of the sea. The look that follows a change of the account's hours, daylight, absence or zone, or of the alert's shortest window, first cuts what was told again, as the sea known until then would have been told under the hours as they are now, and says nothing of it. Hours that the change closed are forgotten, hours that it widened stay told, and hours that it opened elsewhere in the day are told as any new ones: at that look a told window is followed only where it overlaps what is found, and is not said to have moved to hours the change may have opened. Then it reads the forecast as any look does: a window the sea leaves at that same look is still called off after two.
- The time zone and the language are offered by the device with each thing it saves, and taken by an account that has none yet, and by no other: the database decides, so that two devices at once do not take one another's place. A zone that the account chooses is taken as it is.
- A moment that is switched off is not written anywhere: the web app's list has it no more than the mail. Hours that "found" did not tell are not followed, so nothing is said of what becomes of them. With changes switched off, what was told is kept in line with the sea, without a word.

### Windows

- A **sea window** is a run of hours, at one spot, in which every condition is met. It runs from the first such hour to the end of the last.
- What an account can do with it are its **open hours**: the hours of the sea window that are in daylight at the spot when the account asks for daylight, inside the account's hours for that day, and after the date it is away until. They may come in several pieces, each at least as long as the alert's shortest window. A sea window with no such piece is kept in mind and not told.
- An hour of the forecast is open whole or not at all. It is in daylight when most of it is, and inside the account's hours when they cover all of it, wherever the account's midnight falls in it.
- What is told are open hours, piece by piece. A sea window that lasts three days is three mornings to an account that goes in the morning, and each is found, moved or gone for itself. Every piece that qualifies is told: a morning and an evening of one day are two.
- A sea window found at a look continues every window known at that spot that it overlaps, and is as sure as the surest of them: one that splits leaves two that are as sure as it was. Failing an overlap, it continues the nearest known window that nothing else does, when the two begin within six hours of one another.
- What told hours have become is read the same way, from where they were at the last look that found them: the open hours found that overlap them, all of them together, so that an hour's dip in the middle of a morning is no news. Failing an overlap, the nearest open hours that begin within six hours of them and that no other told hours are at. Two told pieces that join become the same hours, and move or go together.
- Hours are counted as time that passes, in the account's time zone: an hour that the clock skips does not exist, and one it repeats counts twice.

### The moments

The worker looks at each alert every three hours: at each of its spots, over the seven days ahead, whatever the account's setting, so that a window already told is followed to its end. It keeps what it saw at each spot, the sea windows in a table of their own and the hours it told beside the spot, and decides from it. A look counts when it comes at least two and a half hours after the one before, whoever runs it and however long its run took to come to the alert: a forecast is fresh for two hours (decision 023), so two looks have read two forecasts, fetched apart. A spot whose forecast is not fresh, or cannot be had, is not looked at: it counts for nothing and against nothing there, and the alert's other spots are looked at without it.

- **Found.** Open hours are told once two looks in a row have found their sea window, they begin within the days the account asks for, and more than half an hour of them is left. Two looks are three hours apart: the model may not have run again between them, and "two forecasts" is as much as an instance that asks one model can say.
- **Moved.** Told hours that now begin or end more than two hours from what was told are told again, by the first look that finds them so. A beginning that has passed counts as now. A smaller move is held against what was told, so that two of them add up.
- **Gone.** Told hours of which two looks in a row find nothing, and that are not over, are called off: the sea window went, or left the account's hours. Hours that were never told go without a word. Hours that come back are new: they are told once their sea window has been found by two looks in a row.
- **The evening before.** At the hour the account chose, one message lists the open hours of the next day, alert by alert and spot by spot: those of the windows two looks have found, and those that were told and that the last look still found. It needs a look no older than six hours. It leaves when its hour has come, or later that evening when the worker was late, and never after midnight. An evening with nothing to list sends nothing. A day is listed once, and a day of which half or more lies in the one listed last is that day: an account that changes its time zone is not sent the same day again.
- **It is on.** When open hours begin, the alert says so, once for each piece, whether the window was told before or not: the moments are switched one by one. Each piece it said so of is remembered, so that hours that appear before others that were said ahead are said too. It is told ahead by as long as the account asked, and no longer once half an hour or less of the piece is left. It needs a look no older than six hours that still found the window.
- An alert on several spots tells them together: one message for an alert, a moment and a look, with a line for each spot. "It is on" is told for the spots that begin in the same quarter of an hour.
- The evening message lists what "found" may have told an hour before. It is the day's recap, not news.

### What the buoy says

- "It is on" comes with the latest reading of the nearest wave station that the sea reaches freely (decision 011), within 60 km of the spot and no older than two hours: its significant height, its peak period and direction, and how old the reading is.
- The message does not say that the buoy confirms the window. A buoy measures the whole sea where it floats, wind sea included, and an alert asks for a swell at a beach: one cannot be held against the other. The reading is given for the reader to judge.
- Far from any buoy the message says the window has begun, and no more.

### What is sent, and how

- A notification is a row that does not change: which alert, which moment, and what it said of which spots and windows. The web app lists them and marks them read. A moment is told once: its row has a key that a second worker, or a retry, cannot write twice.
- For each way out the account chose, a notification has a delivery: when it may leave, until when it is worth sending, and what became of it. Quiet hours set the first: a message made in them leaves at the first moment after them that the clock really shows, when it is set back as when it is set forward. The quiet hours are those the account has when the message leaves: one that changes them, or its time zone, changes when its waiting messages leave.
- A mail is written when it leaves, not when its notification is. For "found", "moved" and "gone", it says how the hours that stand as told at each spot of the alert differ from the hours that mail told there, which are kept apart from what the list told: hours mail never told are new, hours further than two hours from what mail told have moved, and hours that stand no more are gone. So a window that moved while its mail waited is told once, where it is, and one that came and went is not told at all. Several notifications of an alert that waited together leave as one mail, the first of them, and the others have nothing left to say. What a mail says are hours that are open when it leaves, as the last look found them. A spot whose last look is older than six hours, or cut the hours otherwise than the account's settings do now, is left for the next look: the mail says what the other spots have to say, and stays on its way to say the rest. A piece that the last look missed is neither there nor gone: mail says nothing of it until the list does.
- "Moved" and "gone" leave by a way out only when the window was told by it: it follows from the rule above.
- "It is on" and the evening before are read again as they leave: the hours they listed that the last look still found are said as they are now, and a mail with none left is not sent. Nor is any mail of an alert that was switched off or deleted meanwhile, or of a moment that the account switched off. Hours that have become later ones are not "on": the mail waits for them, and says so when they begin. The station's reading is the one of the moment the mail leaves. Two beginnings of an alert that waited together leave as one mail, and what mail said to begin at a spot is kept, so that it is said once. An evening is never sent after midnight: it is given up when the day it lists has begun, on the account's calendar as it is then.
- The worker sends. It is the program that knows when a moment comes, and it now holds the instance's mail settings as the API does. `packages/mail` stays the one way out. This changes decision 026, where only the API sent mail.
- A mail goes to an account whose address is checked, on an instance that sends mail, and that said yes to mail. It is asked with the first alert it makes, and its answer is saved with that alert or not at all. An account whose address is not checked yet is asked too: its mails leave once it is. Otherwise the notification stays in the list.
- A mail leaves in two steps. The first decides what it says, counts it against the day's limits, and writes on the delivery that it is leaving and what it tells: its claim. A limit that is reached holds the mail back there, and it is not tried again: nothing was told, and what it had to say is said by the alert's next mail. The second step holds the account, the alerts the mail speaks of and the account's settings, reads again what the mail was written from and the clock, and hands it to the provider. A mail whose quiet hours began between the two waits for their end. Only then is what it told written to the spots. When a setting, an alert or a look changed between the two, the mail is not handed over: it gives its claim up, nothing was told, and it is written anew.
- A worker that stops between the two steps, or before the provider answers, leaves the claim on the delivery. Whatever else the alert has to say waits for it, and ten minutes later the claim is taken as told: the mail may have left. It is not tried again, and neither is a mail the provider refused. Mail is sent at most once, and may be lost: the list holds what it said. Chosen here.
- An account that stops its mails, that is suspended, or that changes an alert, waits for the mail that is being handed over, ten seconds at most, and no mail leaves once it is answered: a mail that is given up has its connection closed, over SMTP as through a provider's API.
- An account whose address is not checked yet keeps its mails on their way: they leave once it is, while the hours they speak of are ahead.
- A mail that cannot leave yet, for want of a look or of a checked address, is read again a quarter of an hour later, and does not keep those behind it waiting.
- An alert mail has no image, nothing loaded from elsewhere, and no link that counts who followed it, and is drawn as decision 026, "How a mail looks", says, each spot in a panel of its own. It names the alert and the spots, gives the hours in the account's time and units, and the forecast for the first of them as the last look read it, with when that forecast was fetched. It credits the forecast and, for a reading, the station that measured it, and links to the spot and to the settings. A direction is written as a point of the compass with its figure.
- It carries the two headers that let a mail program switch alert mails off in one press, and a link to a page with one button that does the same. The link holds a key that does that and nothing else: it reads nothing and switches nothing on. The key is made when mail is switched on, and anew each time it is switched on again, so that the link of an older mail stops working. It is kept as it is, since the worker writes it into each mail. It is the one thing in the API that needs neither a session nor a key, and it is a `POST`: a program that follows the links of a mail switches nothing off. A `GET` at the address of the headers leads to the page with the button, for a mail program that opens it instead, in the language of the mail, which the address says: nothing is read of the account.
- The log holds counts and kinds of failures. It names no alert, no spot and no address.
- A delivery names its way out: mail, or push, which the next part gives the rules of.

### Push

- A browser that its user lets be notified is a subscription: the address its push service gave it, and its two keys. It belongs to the account that switched push on there. An account that has one is told by push, and one that has none is not: there is no other switch. `alert_settings.by_push` is read by nothing from then on, and is dropped with the columns the release after drops.
- The switch is the device's own, "on this device". Switching it on asks the browser's leave, and switching it off forgets the subscription, there and in the API: the browser lets go first, which stops the notifications whatever the API answers, and a device the API was not told of goes when the app next loads: the browser keeps its id until then, and the address it had. A switch whose answer from the API was lost asks which device has the browser's address, and makes no second one. Only an answer that refuses ends a device: a request that is told to wait, or that gets no answer, changes nothing, and is asked again. The settings list an account's devices, and any of them can be removed from any other. A browser that was removed learns it when its app next loads, and forgets its subscription then. An account keeps ten at most, and an eleventh is refused. Chosen here.
- A browser has one subscription, and it goes with whoever switches push on there: an account that subscribes from a browser takes its subscription from the account before, and signing out forgets it. No one is sent another account's alerts.
- A device is kept with the session of its browser, the one it was made in or last said again in, and goes with it (`push_subscription.session_id`): when the account signs out there, and when the session is closed for it, as an operator's suspension does. The API forgets the device as it ends the session, and a sign-out that did not happen changes nothing. The browser lets go too once its account is out, without waiting for its other tabs, and once more after them: that is what stops the notifications when the API could not end the session. When both fail, a sign-out that the database refused on a page that was closed at once, the device stays until it is removed from another device, or until someone signs in there.
- A device does not end when its session merely runs out. An account that has not opened the app for ninety days, a week until [decision 070](070-signing-in-from-the-app.md), is signed out by the clock, and goes on being told: telling someone who is not looking is what an alert is for. Its device ends when it signs out there, when it is removed, or when its browser lets go. Chosen here, against the other reading, that nobody is told in a browser where nobody is signed in. One case escapes it: a request that still carries the session's cookie once the session ran out has the session deleted, which is the library's doing, and the device goes with it. The cookie runs out as the session does, so it takes a request at that very moment, and push is switched on again there by hand.
- A browser keeps the account its subscription was made for. What another account left there is let go when the app loads, and is never made the device of whoever is signed in: only the switch does that. A browser that does not know whose subscription it holds, and cannot ask, lets go too.
- A page acts for the account it was opened for. What it asks of the API names that account, and is refused when another has signed in from another tab since. The page is behind then: what the browser holds is the other account's own, and the page neither takes it, nor switches it off, nor lets go of it. When no one can say who is signed in, the page is taken to be right, so that no one is sent another account's alerts.
- A browser says its subscription again each time its app loads, since a push service changes an address now and then: its device keeps the new one. It names the device it is, by the id the API gave it, and the API makes none for it then: a device that was removed, or that is another account's now, is answered that it is gone. A browser that kept no id, or one that names none of the account's devices, is the device of the account that has its address. A browser that knows whose device it is, and gets no answer, stays as it is and says so again next time.
- The address is a capability, since whoever holds it can send to that browser: it comes in a request's body, in the field that is its own, it is never logged, and the API never gives it back, in an answer or in a refusal. Nor the two keys. A device is named by an id the API made, and a request that names one by anything else is refused before it is looked for. What a browser is called is its user's own to write, and is given back as it was written. It is also where the worker sends a request, so it is taken only when it is `https` and its host is a push service of the browsers: Google's, Mozilla's, Apple's, Microsoft's. It is kept as a URL spells it, and one with a fragment is refused: a push service is never sent the fragment, so two addresses that differ by theirs are one browser. The browser's two keys are taken when they are of the size the standard gives them, and the public one a point of its curve. The worker checks again as it sends, and follows no redirection. Chosen here.
- An instance sends push when it holds a pair of keys, `VAPID_PUBLIC_KEY` and `VAPID_PRIVATE_KEY`, which a command of the worker makes. The API gives the public one to the web app, and the worker signs with the other. The API needs the public one, and the worker both. An API without it takes no subscription, and the settings say that this instance sends no notification. An API whose public key is no point of its curve stops as it starts: no browser could subscribe with it.
- The web app's service worker does two things: it shows what it is sent, and when a notification is pressed it opens the page that the notification names, if that page is the web app's. It keeps nothing for use offline. It is registered when push is switched on, and not before, and switching push off takes it away.
- What it is sent is a title, a line, the page to open and a tag, so that a newer word on the same thing takes the older one's place. It brings forward a window of the app that is open and leads it to the page, or opens one, and opens the app's own page when the notification names another site's. It takes the windows that are open before it leads one: a window that was opened while the worker was let go of is not its own yet. What it cannot read it shows as a plain line that says an alert has news, in the browser's language, and never drops: a browser that is sent a push and shows nothing takes its leave back.
- The settings say where push stands on the device, in words of its own for each case: a browser that cannot be sent a push, an iPhone whose app is not on the Home Screen, an instance that sends none, a browser whose user said no, a browser that could not be read, off, on, a request on its way, and one that failed. Two tabs of a browser share its one subscription: they take turns to change it, through the browser's locks, and each reads what the browser holds when its user comes back to it. Each answer a tab waits for while it holds the turn, from the API or from what signs accounts in, is given ten seconds. In a browser that has no locks, two tabs that act at the same moment may leave the browser and the API apart until the app next loads.
- An iPhone sends push to a web app only once it is added to the Home Screen. The settings say so there.
- A push leaves as a mail does, by the rules above: when quiet hours end, written as it leaves from what stands as told and from what push told at the spot, which is kept apart from what mail told (`alert_spot.pushed`, `alert_spot.push_begun`), in two steps with its claim, and once at most. A push and a mail of one notification leave apart: each has its delivery, its claim and its count for the day, and neither waits for the other. The worker sends the two at the same time, each with its own share of what a pass hands over: pushes that are many keep no mail waiting, nor mails a push. An account that changes its settings or an alert while a mail and a push of its own are handed over waits for each, ten seconds at most for each. A push asks nothing of the account's address: one that is not checked is pushed all the same.
- A notification gets a delivery for push when a browser of its account is subscribed at that moment. The push goes to every browser the account has when it is handed over: one that subscribed meanwhile is sent it, and one that was removed is not.
- What differs is its size: a title, a line or two, and the page to open. The title is the mail's subject with the alert's name: the setting that keeps names out of a mail's subject is the mail's. Chosen here. It gives the hours of the first two spots, a row for each, and says how many more there are. A spot that an evening lists under several alerts has one row, with each alert's name before its hours. A push of one spot says the sea there in a second row: what a station nearby measured, when "it is on" comes with a reading, and otherwise what the forecast gave for the first of the hours. A name is cut so that the moment and the hours follow it. Pressed, it opens the spot when it speaks of one, and otherwise what the alerts told.
- It carries a tag, which the browser shows it under, so that a newer word takes the place of an older one when it says something of everything the older one spoke of, and never otherwise. A window that push told has a name, where its hours began when they were first told. It keeps it when it moves, told or followed without a word, and when a change of the account's hours cuts it (`since`, in `alert_spot.pushed`), and no two windows of a spot have one name: of the parts of a window that was cut in two, the first keeps it. The tag is the alert, the spots the push speaks of in the order it shows them, and the names of the windows it speaks of at each. So "moved", "gone" and "it is on" are shown where "found" was when they speak of the same windows at the same spots, which is the common case of one window at one spot. A push that speaks of fewer windows than the one before, as "it is on" for the morning after "found" told the morning and the evening, has another tag and stands beside it: replacing it would take the evening off the screen. An evening's tag is its day. Chosen here.
- A push credits no one: it has no room. It gives a few figures of the forecast, or of a station's reading, and leads to the app, where each is credited. Chosen here, and to weigh against what the licences ask.
- It is given to the push service with how long it is worth keeping: until the hours it speaks of are over, as they are when it leaves, and for the evening before until its day begins. A device that was off until then is not told of a window that has gone.
- While a push is handed over, the browsers it goes to are held with the account, its alerts and its settings, in that order and last. A browser that is forgotten, removed, or taken by another account waits for the push that is leaving, ten seconds at most, and none leaves to it once it is answered: a push that is given up has its connection closed.
- A push service that says a subscription is gone has it deleted. A push is sent when one device at least took it. One that no device took is not tried again. What it told depends on whether it can have left: when a push service did not answer, or answered that it failed itself, nobody knows, and the push is taken as told, as a mail is. When every push service said no, it did not leave: nothing was told, and the alert's next push says what this one had to say. When every device is gone the account has no subscription left: the push is given up, and nothing was told. Chosen here.
- An account is sent at most 24 pushes a day, however many devices it has. Chosen here.
- What is sent is encrypted for the browser, as the standards ask (RFC 8291, with RFC 8292 for who sends): the push service reads the address, and nothing of the alert. The instance signs with its private key and names itself by the web app's address. `packages/push` builds this on Node's own cryptography and its own request, with no library: a library's request is the part that had to be ours, as the mail's was over SMTP.

### The tide

Asked on 2026-10-18, after a tester asked for the coefficient, and chosen by Téo among four prototypes the same day: a height, a stage of the tide, a coefficient, and the cycle with a coefficient. He took the last.

- An alert asks the tide by the parts of its cycle, `tideCycle`: one tide from low water to the next, in eight parts, low and high water each one part and each middle stage named with the way the tide goes, "mid tide rising". An hour's part is the fifth of the range of the tide under way, between the turn before it and the turn after, so that it follows how strong the tide is by itself: a height is another stage on a day of big tides. `tideSegmentAt` in `packages/conditions` reads it, and the web's editor draws it as one curve whose parts a press takes or leaves, the app for phones' as a ring ("The editor").
- And by how strong it is, `tideCoefficients`: under 70, from 70 to 90, over 90, read on the high water nearest the hour, within half a day. A coast whose tables print no coefficient meets none, and the editor offers none there.
- The height and the way of the tide stay for the alerts that asked them, and are judged as before: the editor says them, and the cycle takes their place once it is chosen. A new alert is never given them. What an account likes at a place, from the sessions it rated there, is said in the parts of the cycle they were surfed at.
- How long the parts asked last over the two days ahead is measured as the alert is written, and a shortest window longer than that is said never to come, with a button to the longest that fits.

### Or

Asked by Téo on 2026-10-18, from the same tester, who wanted several ranges in one alert. He chose a "+" after each value among three prototypes.

- Each part of an alert may take other values, three at most, under `or`: waves of 1 to 2 m or of 3 m and more. An hour meets the part when it meets one of them, and the alert when it meets every part. The tide's other values are whole asks of the tide, a part of the cycle with its coefficient. Every combination of the values is so judged, without being written.
- What asks nothing is left out as it is saved, an other value as a range with no end, and a set of every word or of none. A part whose own value asks nothing takes its first other one. `askedCriteria` holds it, for the API and the web app.
- A condition that holds windows back is loosened in each of its values. The parts of the cycle and the coefficient have no notch: loosened, the tide asks nothing.

### The editor

Worked out with Téo through a demo on 2026-10-18.

- A new alert is said one part at a time, from "Tell me when the waves are of any size": each part is chosen or passed, and the next opens as it is answered. An alert that is saved, or a copy, is said whole.
- A part opens in a popover on a wide screen, which stays where the part stood while the sentence says what is chosen, and in a drawer over the sheet on a phone. Both say the value big at their top, the count of the week as it was when they opened and what was won or lost since, and end on two buttons: any, which leaves the part asking nothing, and done. An other value offers to remove it instead.
- A part takes a "+" once it has a value, after the last of its values, and none before. An other value says nothing in the sentence until it is chosen, and goes if it is closed empty.
- The sentence ends on "and it lasts at least", the shortest window.
- In the app for phones (decision 069), chosen here: the editor is a sheet of the platform's, and a part opens in a sheet of its own over it, as tall as what it holds, with the controls a finger has. A range is a slider with two thumbs, felt at each step. A direction is a compass whose ends are dragged and whose sector turns whole, with the land shaded and the sectors of an offshore, a cross-offshore and a cross-shore wind offered where the place says where its offshore wind comes from. The cycle of the tide is a ring of eight equal parts, high water at the top and the tide rising up the left, so that each part is a finger's size, with the tide of the two days ahead drawn under it and the hours it asks in color. A new alert goes on to its next part in the same sheet, with "next".
- What the sentence would tell over the week is a line under it, the windows and the days with one, with a dot for each day, which opens on the seven days, each a line of its hours, and a day on its windows, said in the figures the alert asks and no other. The editor asks the preview once for its places, with no condition, which gives every figure of every hour and the hours the account can use, and judges each change itself, by the rules the worker judges by: it asks the API nothing as a slider moves.

### Conditions stay strict

- An hour meets every criterion or does not.
- A likelihood needs a forecast that says how sure it is: several models, or a model's ensemble. The instance asks one model once. When it asks more, an alert may take a lowest likelihood, and a window may come with one.

## Rejected

- **One alert for a spot, and no more.** It was Téo's first answer, and what was built first: fewer things to keep in mind, and fewer messages about the same beach. A spot that works in two kinds of sea could then be told of one only, and he asked for several the day after.
- **Settings on each alert** for the moments, the days ahead and the availability. They describe the person, and a person with eight alerts would set them eight times.
- **Telling a window at first sight**, even one that begins within the day. Téo asked for two forecasts in a row, and the first version called off what it had just announced. A window that appears too late to be found twice is still told when it begins.
- **Saying that the buoy agrees, or does not.** It was the first draft of this decision. A significant height is not a swell height, and a wind sea can lift it over a threshold on a day without swell.
- **A message only when a buoy is near.** A buoy is offshore, sometimes down, and absent from most coasts. Silence would read as "nothing is happening".
- **The API sending the alerts' mail.** It has no clock of its own: it answers requests.

## Consequences

- An alert on a spot far from any tide station never meets a tide criterion (decision 006), and so never speaks. The editor says so before the alert is saved.
- The worker reads the mail settings. An instance that set them for the API alone sends no alert mail until the worker has them: `docs/self-hosting.md` says so.
- The worker reads the pair of keys and the web app's address. An instance whose worker has not both sends no push: what waits for its accounts' browsers is given up when its hours are over.
- A push service refuses a push signed with another pair of keys than the one a browser subscribed with, and does not say that the subscription is gone. An instance that changes its pair keeps its subscriptions, each of which is refused until its browser subscribes again.
- Push in a browser keeps a few gaps that are known and rare. Each takes a failure at one precise moment, or two at once, and each has what ends it. A sign-out that the database refused, on a page that was closed at once, leaves the device: it is removed from another device, or goes when someone signs in there. A page that is behind and cannot ask who is signed in lets go of the other account's subscription, and a browser that lost what it kept while its push service changed its address lets go of its own: push is switched on again there by hand, and the device left in the list goes when its push service says it is gone. A switch whose answers were all lost may say that it failed when the device was made: the next load of the app settles it. A device that was let go while the API could not be told, and whose removal fails again once push is switched on there anew, stays in the list until its push service says it is gone, or until it is removed by hand.
- An alert costs two forecast requests per look for each cell of the model its spots stand in, sixteen a day, and neighbours share a cell (decision 023). Twelve spots along a coast can be twelve cells. Two alerts on one spot read the same forecast, and the second costs no request. The worker looks first at the alerts it looked at longest ago, so that an instance short of requests leaves none behind for good.
- This release is deployed in an order: the API first, and the worker once the API of the version before has stopped. Whatever writes to an alert's spots holds the alert before the spot, as the worker does for the whole of a look, so that two of them never wait for one another. A spot that is in several alerts has them held in the order of their ids, as an evening's mail holds an account's. The worker holds the account before its alert, which is the order an account that is deleted takes them in, and the account's settings before it writes anything of a delivery, as the API does when quiet hours change. The API of the version before deletes a spot the other way round. `docs/self-hosting.md` says so to whoever updates an instance.
- A new alert waits for the worker's next look, up to three hours, and tells what it finds at the one after: its first message comes three to six hours after it was made. The screen that sets it shows what the conditions give meanwhile.
- Every fifteen minutes is how often the worker checks what begins. "It is on" can come up to that much late, and later when the worker is busy or down.
- The time zone is the account's. A spot abroad is told in the account's hours, and its daylight is its own.
- A mail that bounces, or that its reader marks as unwanted, is not known to the instance: it reads no report from its mail provider yet.
- What mail told at a spot is one more thing an alert keeps, `alert_spot.mailed` and `alert_spot.mail_begun`, and so is what push told, `alert_spot.pushed` and `alert_spot.push_begun`. A sea window keeps its hours as the look read them, `alert_window.hours`: "it is on" and the evening before give the sea without reading a forecast. A change of conditions forgets all of it, as it forgets what the list told.
- A look by the version before kept nothing of the sea. Until the worker's first look after an update, which comes within three hours, such a spot is told in the list and not by mail.
- `alert_settings.mail_token_hash` is read by nothing: the key that is kept as it is took its place before any mail was sent. It is dropped by the release that drops the spot's two columns (decision 009).
- The first version's notifications are not carried over. They stay in their table, `notification`, which nothing reads any more and which the release after drops with the spot's two columns. Nothing had been sent. What an alert tells is kept in `alert_notification`.
- Nothing deletes a notification yet. The list shows the newest, and the table grows by a few rows for an alert and a day.
- One model, one run at a time: an alert is as good as that forecast.
